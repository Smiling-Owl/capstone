# 1.5 Significance of the Project

The proposed Online Disaster Situation Report Generation and Record Management System is significant as a shared, startup-operated service that can support more than one disaster risk reduction and management (DRRM) organization without transferring public authority to the private provider. Its value lies in providing a consistent structure for local incident records, verification, SitRep preparation, retrieval, and accountability while allowing every participating organization to retain control over its jurisdiction, users, records, approvals, and official submissions. The benefits described below are expected contributions to be tested; they are not guaranteed outcomes of the prototype.

**Purok personnel and other authorized local reporters.** The system can give frontline reporters a consistent way to record the location, time, observed effects, affected population, immediate needs, actions taken, source, and verification status of an incident. Required fields, explicit unknown values, and correction histories may reduce the loss of context that occurs when observations are scattered across paper records or informal messages. The module does not replace radio, telephone, or face-to-face reporting during connectivity failures, and the usefulness of each record still depends on timely, careful, and truthful data entry.

**Barangay personnel and BDRRMCs.** Barangay personnel may use the platform to review reports from their local areas, distinguish preliminary information from verified information, consolidate related records, document corrections, and forward authorized reports through an established workflow. A traceable record of who submitted, reviewed, returned, validated, or rejected information can make follow-up work clearer and help prevent material corrections from being hidden by file replacement. These benefits depend on appropriate account assignment, training, local verification procedures, and continued use of approved contingency channels.

**Participating DRRM organizations and local decision-makers.** Provincial, city, municipal, barangay, and other authorized DRRM organizations may configure their own users, jurisdictions, reporting routes, and local SitRep templates within a common service. Structured and geographically attributable records can support the consolidation, comparison, and retrieval duties assigned to local DRRM institutions under Republic Act No. 10121. The SitRep Generation Module may reduce repeated encoding by mapping eligible validated records to applicable NDRRM Operations Center categories, while the dashboard and catalog may help authorized personnel monitor report status and retrieve previous records. The system supports these tasks but does not determine whether an incident is true, approve an official SitRep, declare a state of calamity, allocate resources, or replace the judgment of authorized officials.

**The startup and platform personnel.** The project provides a basis for evaluating whether one maintained application can serve multiple DRRM organizations through configurable organization boundaries rather than separate custom systems for every customer. A shared service may concentrate hosting, maintenance, security updates, backups, restoration, support, and software improvements. It also creates responsibilities: customer environments must be isolated; support access must be exceptional and logged; incidents and outages require accountable response; and customers need usable export and transition arrangements. Prototype completion or favorable usability results will not alone establish commercial viability, procurement eligibility, demand, affordability, or production readiness.

**Affected communities.** Communities may benefit indirectly when authorized personnel can locate, verify, consolidate, and retrieve local incident information more consistently. Preserving the source, jurisdiction, time, status, and revision history of a report may help officials identify what is known, what remains uncertain, and which local areas require follow-up. However, software use does not guarantee faster rescue, equitable assistance, reduced casualties, or better decisions. Those outcomes also depend on connectivity, staffing, verification, logistics, institutional coordination, resources, and the lawful use of information. Collection should remain proportionate because detailed demographic or casualty data can expose individuals to privacy, safety, discrimination, or re-identification risks if accessed or disclosed improperly.

**Records officers, system administrators, and data-protection personnel.** The platform can support accountability by associating records with an organization, jurisdiction, actor, timestamp, verification state, version, retention classification, and audit event. Role and object authorization, organization-scoped queries, controlled support access, backups, restoration testing, and export functions can assist security and continuity work. These technical controls complement rather than replace the lawful-purpose, transparency, proportionality, privacy, records-management, breach-response, retention, and authorized-disposition duties established by Republic Act No. 10173 and Republic Act No. 9470. Government records remain under the custody and records-management authority of the responsible public office even when the startup hosts the service.

**Future researchers and system developers.** The study can provide a documented case of translating Philippine DRRM reporting responsibilities into a configurable multi-organization information system. Its requirements, workflow decisions, test scenarios, and findings may inform later research on disaster-information quality, public-sector software adoption, accessible emergency information systems, privacy, interoperability, service continuity, and provider exit. Historical records may support later analysis only when their provenance, definitions, verification status, lawful research basis, and limitations are preserved. The project does not include predictive analytics or machine learning, and future researchers should not treat administrative records as representative of unreported events or populations with weaker connectivity.

Operationally, the project may improve the consistency and traceability of the reporting process by keeping submissions, decisions, corrections, source versions, and generated SitReps in one authorized workflow. Research on disaster decision-making identifies completeness, validity, reliability, accuracy, currency, accessibility, interpretability, and consistency as distinct dimensions of information quality; a form that is complete is not necessarily accurate or current (Jayawardene et al., 2021). Evaluation should therefore report the dimensions actually measured, such as field completeness, valid-value rate, correction frequency, verification turnaround, aggregation accuracy, and source traceability, rather than claiming that centralization automatically produces reliable information.

Technically, the project demonstrates responsive browser access, role-based permissions, organization isolation, structured records, versioned corrections, SitRep consolidation, searchable archives, dashboard summaries, audit logs, backups, restoration, and customer export in one prototype. Its multi-organization design is relevant to the E-Governance Act's direction toward accessible, secure, interoperable, and continuity-aware government processes and its recognition of collaboration with private entities and startups. Alignment with those policy directions does not by itself establish statutory compliance, certification, integration with a government platform, or authorization to exchange records (Republic Act No. 12254, 2025).

Economically, a shared service may avoid developing and maintaining a completely separate application for each participating organization. Common hosting, updates, training materials, and support may reduce duplicated effort, while configurable workflows may allow organizations to retain necessary local differences. Whether the service is actually affordable or cost-effective can be determined only after accounting for development, hosting, connectivity, support, training, security, procurement, contract management, data migration, export, and provider-transition costs. The prototype therefore establishes a system concept and evaluation evidence, not a return-on-investment claim.

From a governance perspective, the platform can make responsibility more explicit. Each participating organization remains responsible for its lawful purpose, local users, jurisdiction, verification, approval, disclosure, official submission, records custody, retention, and disposition. The startup is responsible for the technical service it agrees to provide, including hosting, maintenance, security controls, monitoring, backups, recovery, support, portability, and transition assistance. Electronic approval evidence may preserve the approver's identity, role, organization, time, and report version, but acceptance of an electronic output remains subject to the receiving agency's prescribed procedure and the reliability requirements of Republic Act No. 8792.

The significance of the project ultimately depends on representative participation, accurate source data, timely human verification, reliable connectivity and hosting, accessible interfaces, training, clear policies, and sustained organizational ownership. Evaluation must distinguish an implemented capability from a measured result and a measured result from a wider disaster-response outcome. It should also document possible burdens or harms, including duplicate encoding, approval bottlenecks, notification overload, inaccessible tasks, cross-organization exposure, inappropriate support access, and dependence on the service provider. This evidence is necessary to determine whether the startup-operated platform is suitable for broader pilot use and what must be improved before production adoption.
